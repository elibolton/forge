# Rep Ranges/Load, Proximity to Failure (RIR), Rest Intervals, Exercise Order, Warm-ups, and Session Time Budgeting

Scope note: Sources were found with WebSearch and abstract/press-level summaries. Several full texts (Robinson 2024, Refalo 2024, Nunes 2021) returned 403 or cookie walls, so effect sizes are only reported where an abstract or repository page gave them. Confidence levels: **High** = several meta-analyses agree; **Moderate** = one meta-analysis or a few RCTs, some heterogeneity; **Low** = sparse evidence or practitioner guidance only. Encodable numbers in the Inferences sections are the author's synthesis unless they are cited.

## 1. Load / rep range for hypertrophy vs strength

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy is roughly load-independent across about 6–30+ reps when sets are taken close to failure. Maximal strength gains are larger with heavy loads (about ≥80% 1RM, about ≤8 reps), and moderate loads (9–15 reps) still beat low loads for strength. Confidence: **High** for both statements.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (JSCR) meta-analysis of 21 studies compared ≤60% 1RM with >60% 1RM. Hypertrophy changes were similar between conditions, and maximal strength gains were greater with high loads. — [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- Lopez et al. 2021 (MSSE) network meta-analysis: 28 studies, 747 healthy adults, all trained to volitional failure. It compared low load (>15 reps), moderate load (9–15 reps) and high load (≤8 reps). Hypertrophy was load-independent. Strength was best with high load, and both high and moderate loads beat low load (abstract-summary SMDs of about 0.60–0.63 vs about 0.34–0.35; not verified against the full text). Gains were larger in untrained people, and in trained people training more sessions per week. — [PubMed 33433148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/); [JYX repository](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_75929)
- Grgic et al. 2020 found no significant difference between low-load and high-load training in type I or type II fiber hypertrophy. — [PubMed 33312275](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33312275/)
- Currier et al. 2023 (BJSM) Bayesian network meta-analysis (strength: 178 studies, n=5097; hypertrophy: 119 studies, n=3364):
  - Every prescription beat the control.
  - Higher-load (>80% 1RM) prescriptions maximized strength.
  - All prescriptions promoted hypertrophy comparably. The top-ranked hypertrophy option was higher-load, multi-set training twice weekly.
  - Threshold analysis showed the results were robust. The authors suggest choosing by preference and adherence.
  - Sources: [PMC10579494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579494/); [BJSM PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/18/1211.full.pdf)
- ACSM 2026 Position Stand ("Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews") synthesized 137 systematic reviews (>30,000 participants) and replaces the 2009 stand. A secondary summary reports:
  - Strength: about 80% 1RM, 2–3 sets per exercise.
  - Hypertrophy: about 10 weekly sets per muscle.
  - Power: 30–70% 1RM with fast concentrics.
  - Training each major muscle group at least 2x/week matters more than program details.
  - These numbers come from secondary coverage and were not verified in the full text.
  - Sources: [ACSM summary](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/amp/articles/new-resistance-training-guidelines-debunk-myths-stronger-muscles-strength-size); [Rep Fitness summary](https://repfitness.com/blogs/training/acsm-updated-resistance-training-guidelines)

### Inferences
Suggested generator defaults. This is a synthesis: the rep numbers are chosen within the evidence-supported ranges, and the exact cut-points are design choices.

| Goal | Compound (main lifts) | Compound (secondary) | Isolation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Get stronger (general strength) | 3–6 reps (~80–90% 1RM) | 6–10 | 8–15 |
| Powerlifting | 1–5 reps (~80–92%+) on squat/bench/deadlift (singles/doubles for advanced) | 4–8 | 8–15 |
| Build muscle | 6–10 | 8–12 | 10–20 |
| Get lean / weight loss (preserve muscle while dieting) | 6–10 | 8–12 | 10–15 |
| General fitness | 6–12 | 8–12 | 10–15 |

- **Isolation exercises:** use higher reps (10–20). Hypertrophy does not depend on load, and heavy low-rep isolation work adds joint stress and is hard to load in small increments. The isolation rep range is an inference.
- **Beginners:** use 8–12+ reps on compounds even when the goal is strength. Lopez 2021 found that untrained people gain regardless of load, and moderate loads are easier to learn technique with. This is an inference; the evidence specific to beginners comes from Lopez's untrained subgroup.
- **Weight-loss and get-lean goals:** the evidence does not support a special "toning" rep range. Use the same loads as hypertrophy work, and adjust volume and rest for time. This is an inference.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the full-text effect sizes for Currier 2023 or Lopez 2021.
- I did not access the ACSM 2026 full text, so the %1RM figures come from secondary summaries.
- I found no meta-analysis that sets rep ranges separately for compound and isolation exercises.

## 2. Proximity to failure (RIR)

### Takeaway
- **Hypertrophy:** growth rises as sets end closer to failure, probably non-linearly. Strength gains are largely insensitive to RIR across a wide range. Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **Trained lifters:** stopping at 1–2 RIR produced quad growth similar to training to failure, with less fatigue. Confidence: **Moderate**, based on a single RCT.
- **Average healthy adults:** ACSM 2026 says routine training to failure is not required. Confidence: **Moderate–High**.

### Cited Findings
- Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med 53:649–665) meta-analysis of 15 studies:
  - Failure vs non-failure produced a small, borderline advantage for failure (ES=0.19, 95% CI 0.00–0.37, p=0.045).
  - The advantage disappeared when studies were split by how they defined non-failure.
  - High vs moderate velocity-loss thresholds showed no difference (ES=0.08, p=0.529).
  - The authors suggest a non-linear relationship. A 2024 commentary (Fonseca et al.) critiqued the paper.
  - Sources: [PubMed 36334240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01946-6)
- Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos 2024 (Sports Med; DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2) ran exploratory multilevel meta-regressions with RIR as a continuous variable:
  - Only hypertrophy was meaningfully influenced by RIR.
  - Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR.
  - The authors caution that RIR estimates are imprecise and the analysis is exploratory.
  - Sources: [Abertay repository](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295); [News-Medical summary](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20240801/New-study-reveals-sweet-spot-for-muscle-growth.aspx)
- Refalo et al. 2024 (J Sports Sci; PMID 38393985) within-subject RCT, resistance-trained people, 8 weeks:
  - One leg trained to momentary failure; the other stopped at about 2 RIR, then 1 RIR.
  - Quad hypertrophy was similar.
  - Failure caused consistently higher acute neuromuscular fatigue and greater rep loss across sets.
  - 1–2 RIR allowed similar volume load.
  - The authors note results may vary with exercise selection.
  - Sources: [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/dbe641c7-a606-4e00-94bc-bb2fbe253afa)
- ACSM 2026: training to fatigue or momentary failure "did not consistently impact outcomes" for healthy adults. An ACSM-affiliated commentator warned that frequent failure training raises fatigue and injury risk. — [ACSM](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/amp/articles/new-resistance-training-guidelines-debunk-myths-stronger-muscles-strength-size)

### Inferences
Suggested RIR defaults (synthesis; not directly prescribed by any single source):

| Goal / level | Heavy compounds | Isolation / machines |
|---|---|---|
| Build muscle, intermediate/advanced | 1–3 RIR | 0–2 RIR; last set may go to failure |
| Build muscle, beginner | 2–4 RIR | 1–3 RIR |
| Get stronger / powerlifting | 2–4 RIR (strength is RIR-insensitive, so spare fatigue); top singles about 1–2 RIR | 1–3 RIR |
| General fitness / weight loss / get lean | 2–3 RIR | 1–3 RIR |

- **Rationale:**
  - Hypertrophy benefits from training close to failure (Robinson 2024).
  - Strength does not need it (Robinson 2024).
  - Failure adds fatigue (Refalo 2024).
  - Failure on axially loaded free-weight compounds carries more technical and safety risk than on machines or isolations. This last point is practitioner reasoning, not directly tested in these sources.
- **Last set to failure:** reasonable as an option on isolation and machine exercises for hypertrophy. It is not needed for strength.
- **Beginners and RIR:** beginners judge RIR poorly. Robinson et al. note RIR imprecision in general, so a higher target RIR gives them a buffer. This is an inference.

### Gaps
- I could not access the slope estimates from Robinson 2024 (effect per RIR, number of studies) because of paywall and cookie walls. Do not encode a numeric per-RIR effect.
- I did not directly retrieve Helms et al.'s RIR-based RPE scale papers on RIR rating accuracy by training age.
- No reviewed meta-analysis splits RIR effects by compound vs isolation.

## 3. Rest intervals

### Takeaway
- **Hypertrophy:** rest longer than 60 s gives a small advantage, and there is no detectable added hypertrophy beyond about 90 s (Singer 2024). Confidence: **Moderate**; there is substantial heterogeneity and the credible intervals include zero.
- **Strength:** longer rests (about 3 min or more) favor strength. Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **What short rest costs:** mainly lost volume load. If volume is equalized (for example with extra sets), short rest's hypertrophy deficit appears to shrink. Confidence: **Low–Moderate**.

### Cited Findings
- Singer et al. 2024, "Give it a rest" (Front Sports Act Living; Bayesian meta-analysis):
  - 9 studies, 19 measurements.
  - Small hypertrophic benefit for rest >60 s, likely via volume load.
  - No appreciable difference when resting >90 s.
  - Failure vs non-failure did not modify the effect.
  - Heterogeneity was substantial.
  - Sources: [PMC11349676](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11349676/); [PubMed 39205815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/)
- Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (JSCR) RCT:
  - 21 trained men, 8 weeks, 3 sessions per week, 3 sets of 8–12RM on 7 exercises.
  - 3-min rest gave greater strength and hypertrophy than 1-min rest.
  - Schoenfeld's blog describes the short condition differently from the paper, so check the full text.
  - Sources: [PubMed 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/); [Schoenfeld blog](https://www.lookgreatnaked.com/blog/what-is-the-ideal-rest-interval-for-muscle-growth-implications-from-our-recent-study)
- Grgic et al. 2017 (Eur J Sport Sci) systematic review of 6 studies comparing ≤60 s with >60 s rest:
  - Results were equivocal overall, with a possible advantage of long rest in trained participants.
  - As described by Singer 2024, both short and long rest are viable for untrained people, and longer rest may help experienced lifters.
  - Source: [PubMed 28641044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)
- Longo et al. 2022: volume load, not the rest interval itself, drove hypertrophy. When volume load was matched, short rest performed similarly. — [PubMed 35622106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35622106/)
- de Salles et al. 2010 (trained men): 3–5 min rest gave greater upper- and lower-body strength gains than 1 min. — [PubMed 19811949](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19811949/)
- ACSM 2026 coverage lists rest among variables that do not consistently change outcomes much. I found no specific number. — [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/amp/articles/new-resistance-training-guidelines-debunk-myths-stronger-muscles-strength-size)

### Inferences
Suggested rest defaults in seconds, between sets of the same exercise:

| Goal | Heavy compound | Secondary compound | Isolation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Powerlifting | 180–300 | 120–180 | 60–120 |
| Get stronger | 150–240 | 120–180 | 60–90 |
| Build muscle | 120–180 | 90–150 | 60–90 |
| General fitness | 90–150 | 90–120 | 60–90 |
| Get lean / weight loss | 90–120 | 60–90 | 45–75 (or supersets) |

- **Why compounds get longer rest:** they use more total muscle mass and are more systemically fatiguing, so they need more time to recover rep performance. This is practitioner reasoning. Singer 2024 did not split results by exercise type.
- **Beginners:** about 60–90 s is acceptable, because short and long rest are both viable in untrained people (Grgic 2017).
- **Time-saving mode:** if the generator shortens rest to save time, it should add about one set per exercise or use antagonist supersets to preserve volume load (Longo 2022 rationale). This is an inference.
- **Auto-regulation:** let users start the next set when ready, but no sooner than the floor values.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis that separates rest needs for compound vs isolation exercises.
- Rest evidence for women, older adults and beginners is thin.
- The ACSM 2026 rest-interval numbers were not retrieved.

## 4. Exercise order

### Takeaway
- **Strength:** gains are largest in exercises performed early in the session, so put priority lifts first. Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **Hypertrophy:** order has no significant effect, so ordering compound-first vs isolation-first is flexible for growth. Confidence: **Moderate**, from 11 small studies.

### Cited Findings
- Nunes et al. 2021 (Eur J Sport Sci 21:149–157) meta-analysis, 11 studies, 268 participants:
  - Pooling all strength tests, there was no overall order effect (ES=−0.11, p=0.306).
  - Strength gains were largest in the exercises done at the start of the session.
  - Order had no effect on hypertrophy (ES=0.03, p=0.862).
  - Hypertrophy measures mixed site-specific and indirect methods.
  - Sources: [PubMed 32077380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/); [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/44202/)

### Inferences
Suggested generator ordering rules:
1. Power or explosive work comes first, if any.
2. Next, the goal's main heavy compound. For powerlifting this is the competition lift of the day.
3. Then the remaining compounds, larger before smaller.
4. Then isolations.
5. Core and conditioning go last.

- **Priority-muscle override:** a user-flagged priority muscle or lift can move to the start. Nunes found order matters for the strength of that lift and not for hypertrophy. This is an inference.
- **Safety:** high-skill or axially loaded lifts (squat, deadlift, Olympic variants) should not come after exhausting isolations of the same muscles. This is practitioner reasoning.

### Gaps
- Most order studies used untrained or recreationally trained people over 6–12 weeks.
- The direct hypertrophy evidence for large compound-lift muscles is limited.

## 5. Warm-up sets

### Takeaway
A few progressively heavier ramp-up sets before the first working set of a heavy compound are reasonable. Practitioner sources range from 2 to 5 sets. Isolation exercises done after a related compound need 0–1 warm-up sets. Confidence: **Low** (practitioner guidance; I did not retrieve primary RCTs).

### Cited Findings
- Coaching sources recommend gradually increasing load over 2–4 sets before the first heavy set. Some suggest about 2 warm-up sets are enough for bodybuilding work, and warn to keep ramp sets minimal to avoid fatigue. — [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/warm-up-before-lifting/); [BarBend](https://barbend.com/ramp-up-sets/)
- A trainer's example scheme for big compounds uses about 50% and 75% of working load, then a single rep at about 110%. — [Breaking Muscle](https://breakingmuscle.com/shred-telligent-design-how-to-ramp-up-your-major-lifts/)
- Stronger By Science discusses evidence that a heavier warm-up set can improve subsequent hypertrophy-set performance more than a lighter one. — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61279)
- Secondary sources cite reviews that warm-up improves performance and 1RM strength. I could not verify these. — [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/warm-up-before-lifting/)

### Inferences
Suggested warm-up-set defaults (practitioner-based; flag as such in the app):

| Exercise | Warm-up sets | Scheme |
|---|---|---|
| First heavy compound, strength or powerlifting | 3–5 | e.g. bar×8–10, 40%×5, 60%×3, 75%×2, 85–90%×1 of the working weight. More sets as the working load rises. |
| First compound, hypertrophy / general / fat-loss | 2–3 | about 50%×8, 70%×4, 85%×2 |
| Later compound for already warmed muscles | 1–2 | |
| Isolation, or a machine following a compound for the same muscle | 0–1 | |
| Bodyweight or very light movements | 0 | |
| Beginners | 1–2 on compounds | Their working loads are light |

Warm-up reps should be low and far from failure, and rest between warm-up sets should be short (about 30–90 s, longer for the last near-working-weight singles).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve RCTs or meta-analyses on the optimal number of specific warm-up sets.
- The 3–5 set claim on BarBend cites no studies.

## 6. Time per working set and transitions (session-length budgeting)

### Takeaway
Repetition durations from 0.5 to 8 s give similar hypertrophy, and reps over 10 s are inferior. A realistic per-rep time for timing estimates is about 2.5–4 s, so a set takes roughly reps × 3 s plus 5–10 s of setup. Confidence: **Low–Moderate** for the time constants (an inference from rep-duration ranges). I found no study measuring real-world gym set or transition times.

### Cited Findings
- Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2015 (Sports Med) meta-analysis, 8 studies:
  - Repetition durations of 0.5–8 s produce similar hypertrophy when sets go to failure.
  - Very slow reps over 10 s appear inferior, though the data are limited.
  - Sources: [PubMed 25601394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0304-0)
- Schoenfeld 2016 implemented 3 sets × 7 exercises × 3 sessions per week, a concrete example of a full-body session structure. — [PubMed 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)

### Inferences
These are encodable time-budget formulas built from the rep-duration evidence plus practitioner assumptions. Treat them as tunable constants, ideally calibrated against the app's own logged timestamps.

**Constants:**
- `sec_per_rep` = 3 (range 2.5–4; use 4 for heavy low-rep sets where bracing between reps takes longer).
- `set_overhead` = 10 s (unracking, setup, re-racking).
- `working_set_time` = reps × sec_per_rep + set_overhead.
  - 5 reps → about 25–30 s; 10 reps → about 40 s; 15 reps → about 55 s.
- Unilateral exercises: double the rep time (each side), or add a short rest between sides.
- `warmup_set_time` = about 20 s of lifting + 45 s of rest/plate change, so about 60–75 s per warm-up set.
  - 3 warm-up sets ≈ 3–4 min; 5 warm-up sets ≈ 5–6 min.
- `transition_time` between exercises = 90–120 s (moving stations, adjusting the machine, loading plates). Use 60 s for dumbbell or cable stations next to each other, and about 180 s for a barbell setup with heavy plate loading.

**Formulas:**
- Per exercise:
  `T_ex = warmups × warmup_set_time + sets × working_set_time + (sets − 1) × rest + transition_time`
- Supersets or circuits (two exercises A+B):
  `T = sets × (set_A + set_B + short_transition ≈ 15 s) + (sets − 1) × rest_after_pair`.
  Rest between rounds is typically 90–120 s.
- Add a general warm-up of about 5 min if the app includes one.

**Worked examples:**

| Example | Warm-ups | Working sets | Rests | Transition | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy compound, 3×8, 150 s rest | 2 × 70 s = 140 s | 3 × 34 s = 102 s | 2 × 150 s = 300 s | 120 s | ≈ 11 min |
| Hypertrophy isolation, 3×12, 75 s rest | 0 | 3 × 46 s = 138 s | 2 × 75 s = 150 s | 90 s | ≈ 6.3 min |
| Powerlifting main lift, 5×3, 240 s rest | 5 × 75 s = 375 s | 5 × 22 s = 110 s | 4 × 240 = 960 s | 180 s | ≈ 27 min |

- **Rule of thumb:** a 60-min hypertrophy session fits about 2 compounds plus 3–4 isolations at 3 sets each, or about 16–20 working sets. A 45-min session fits about 12–15 working sets.
- **Budget-trimming order** when a session runs over the target length:
  1. Shorten isolation rest toward the floor values.
  2. Pair non-competing isolations into supersets.
  3. Drop the last set of isolations.
  4. Drop an exercise.
  5. Only then cut compound rest.

  This order is an inference. It is based on short rest costing mostly volume load (Longo 2022) and on compounds being more rest-sensitive, which is practitioner reasoning.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed data on typical gym transition times or setup overhead. All transition constants are assumptions.
- I found no studies measuring actual rep cadence in self-paced lifters for this note.
- Use the app's own logged set timestamps to calibrate `sec_per_rep` and `transition_time` per user.
