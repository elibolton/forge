# Time-Efficient Resistance-Training Techniques and Progression/Periodization Models for an Automatic Workout Generator

Scope note: about 17 search/fetch calls. PMC full texts were blocked by a CAPTCHA and the Springer full text needed a login. Most figures below therefore come from abstracts, repository pages and search-result summaries of the primary papers. Confidence ratings (High / Moderate / Low) are my own judgment of how well the evidence supports encoding each rule.

## (a) Time-efficient techniques: when to use supersets, drop sets, rest-pause, myo-reps and circuits, how much time they save, and what they cost

### Takeaway
Supersets (especially agonist-antagonist or non-competing pairs), drop sets and rest-pause all keep hypertrophy, and largely strength, about the same as traditional sets while cutting session time by roughly half. The costs: higher perceived exertion and lactate, and a lower volume load when the paired exercises compete for the same muscles. Iversen et al. 2021 judge these methods better for hypertrophy than for maximal strength, so a generator should prefer them for hypertrophy/general goals and accessory work, not for the main strength lifts of a strength-focused user.

### Cited Findings
**General framework (Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland 2021, Sports Med 51:2079–2095)**
- A narrative review aimed at the general public with limited training time, explicitly not athletes optimizing without regard to time — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)
- Weekly volume matters more than frequency. Minimum of ≥4 weekly sets per muscle group at a 6–15RM load; 15–40 reps can be used if sets go to volitional failure — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)
- Supersets, drop sets and rest-pause can roughly halve session time while keeping volume similar to traditional training. They are "probably better at inducing hypertrophy than muscular strength" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)
- Prioritize bilateral, multi-joint, full-ROM exercises and limit warm-up to exercise-specific warm-up sets — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)
- Carlson, Smith & Fisher (2022 letter) argue the time-efficient recommendations should extend to athletes too — [MMU e-space](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/629394/1/Letter%20to%20Editor%20Iverson_FINAL_after%20revisions.pdf)

**Supersets: meta-analysis (Zhang, Weakley, García-Ramos et al. 2025, Sports Med; 19 studies, n=313)**
- Supersets shorten session duration without cutting total volume. Large efficiency effect, SMD = 1.74 — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/); [Leeds Beckett repository](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)
- Chronic adaptations: similar maximal strength, strength endurance and hypertrophy vs traditional sets (hypertrophy SMD = −0.05, p = 0.87) — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)
- Costs: higher RPE (SMD = 0.77, p = 0.02) and higher blood lactate during and after training — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)
- Subgroups: agonist-antagonist supersets allowed *more* reps than traditional sets. "Similar-biomechanics" (agonist/same-muscle) supersets produced *lower* volume load — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)
- Higher energy cost with supersets — [Leeds Beckett PDF](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908/1/SupersetVersusTraditionalResistanceTrainingPrescriptionsASystematicReviewAndMetaanalysisExploringAcuteAndChronicPV-WEAKLEY.pdf)
- Critique: Mang, Beam & Kravitz (2025) say the meta-analysis gave limited practical programming guidance — [Frontiers review listing](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1549609)

**Supersets and tri-sets: acute study (Weakley et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol)**
- Efficiency was defined as kg lifted per minute. Supersets and tri-sets improved efficiency and cut training time. Tri-sets had greater efficiency and RPE, but session-RPE was very likely lower; lactate was higher for supersets and tri-sets — [Leeds Beckett](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/3890/6/Weakley%20et%20al.pdf); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-017-3680-3)

**Agonist-antagonist paired sets (Robbins et al. 2010)**
- Complex/paired sets (bench pull alternated with bench press, 18 trained men) had about 2× the efficiency (output per time) of traditional sets, with no difference in volume load per set or per session — [PubMed 20555283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20555283/)
- A paired-set study (JSCR 24(5)) found paired sets kept volume load as well as traditional sets and were more efficient per unit of time — [QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/20847705/the-effect-of-an-upper-body-agonist-antagonist-resistance-training-protocol-on-volume-load-and-efficiency)
- An 8-week training study: complex-set sessions took about half the time, with similar bench pull and bench press 1RM gains — [PubMed 19967584](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19967584/)
- Robbins' 2010 review: acute performance-enhancement use of paired sets is limited, but they may have merit as a time-effective way to build strength — [PubMed 20733520](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20733520/)
- I did not retrieve the Paz et al. antagonist superset studies (see Gaps).

**Drop sets**
- Sødal et al. 2023 (Sports Med Open; 6 studies, 5 meta-analysed): no significant hypertrophy difference between drop sets and traditional sets. Drop sets are "an efficient strategy" for people short on time — [PMC10390395](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10390395/); [PubMed 37523092](https://www.pubmed.gov/37523092/)
- Coleman et al. 2022 meta-analysis (Int J Strength Cond): trivial strength difference ES 0.07 (95% CI −0.14 to 0.29) and a trivial hypertrophy difference — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/46296/)
- A newer 2026 meta-analysis (Sports Med Open, 12 studies): drop sets give comparable long-term hypertrophy and strength and are a time-efficient alternative — [Springer s40798-026-01012-1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01012-1)

**Rest-pause and myo-reps**
- Prestes et al. (JSCR, trained subjects, 6 weeks): rest-pause gave greater thigh thickness (11 ± 14% vs 1 ± 7%) and leg-press rep endurance (27 ± 8% vs 8 ± 2%) than traditional multiple sets. A secondary source reports no strength difference — [PubMed 28617715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28617715/). Note: the publication-year attribution (2017 epub vs 2019 print) was inconsistent across sources.
- Enes et al. 2021 (Appl Physiol Nutr Metab): rest-pause and drop sets gave similar strength and hypertrophy adaptations vs traditional sets in trained males. One secondary source reports a greater back-squat 1RM gain for an experimental group (p = 0.001), but I could not verify that against the full paper — [PubMed 34260860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260860/)
- Myo-reps: an 8-week trial in trained men reportedly found similar strength and size gains vs traditional training, with ~29% fewer reps in ~62% less time (PRACTITIONER secondary source; authors not named and the framing was questioned by the source's own writer) — [Biolayne Reps issue 50](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-50/myo-reps-for-mass/)

**Advanced techniques overall (Krzysztofik et al. 2019, IJERPH; 30 studies)**
- Covers eccentric tempo, accentuated eccentrics, BFR, cluster sets, supersets/pre-exhaustion and drop sets. Positions advanced methods as tools for well-trained lifters to break plateaus and reduce monotony, and notes a lack of consensus on their efficiency vs traditional approaches — [PubMed 31817252](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31817252/); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/16/24/4897)

### Inferences
Candidate rules for the generator:
- **Rule A1: default to supersets when time is limited (High confidence).** Pair antagonists (push/pull, e.g. bench + row, curl + triceps extension) or non-competing exercises (e.g. upper + lower). Expect roughly 40–50% time savings with no loss in volume or chronic adaptation (Zhang 2025; Robbins 2010; Iversen 2021).
- **Rule A2: avoid same-muscle/agonist supersets unless pre-exhaustion is the goal (Moderate-High).** They lower volume load (Zhang 2025 subgroup).
- **Rule A3: keep heavy main lifts as straight sets for strength-goal users (Moderate).** Do not superset the primary low-rep compound, or pair it only with a non-competing, low-fatigue movement. Iversen 2021 says these methods favour hypertrophy over max strength. Strength outcomes in the supersets meta-analysis were similar, so this is a cautious default rather than a firm finding.
- **Rule A4: use drop sets on the last set of isolation/machine exercises (Moderate-High).** Hypertrophy is equivalent and they save time (Sødal 2023; Coleman 2022). Best on machines and dumbbells where the load can be changed quickly; avoid them on heavy barbell compounds (a practical/safety inference, not tested).
- **Rule A5: rest-pause or myo-reps can replace 2–3 straight sets of an isolation exercise (Moderate for rest-pause, Low-Moderate for myo-reps).** Evidence is a few small trials plus practitioner sources.
- **Rule A6: account for perceived exertion (Moderate).** Dense techniques raise RPE and lactate (Zhang 2025), so cap how many are used per session for beginners and for users reporting high fatigue. The exact cap is a design choice; no study gives a number.
- **Rule A7: circuits/tri-sets are the most time-dense option (Moderate).** Weakley 2017 found the highest kg/min, but they raise RPE, so suit them to general-fitness goals.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the Paz et al. antagonist superset studies or exact per-technique time-savings percentages from the Iversen full text (PMC/Springer were blocked).
- I found no RCT comparing non-competing (e.g. upper/lower) supersets with antagonist supersets on chronic outcomes. The "non-competing" benefit is inferred from mechanism plus the agonist-vs-antagonist subgroup result.
- I found no evidence-based cap on the number of drop sets or rest-pause sets per session.
- The myo-reps primary study could not be identified or verified; the evidence comes from a practitioner source.
- I found no long-term circuit-training RCTs in trained populations in this pass.

## (b) Progression, autoregulation, deloads, periodization and exercise variation

### Takeaway
Both load progression and rep progression (i.e. double progression) work. RIR/RPE autoregulation is at least as good as fixed percentages and may slightly favour strength. Periodization gives a small-to-moderate strength advantage, but no hypertrophy advantage when volume is equated. Undulating beats linear for strength only in trained lifters. A 1-week full layoff "deload" mid-block did not help and slightly hurt strength. Practitioners deload about every 5–6 weeks by cutting volume while keeping frequency and exercises. Exercise variation should be systematic and modest, not random.

### Cited Findings
**Progression: load vs reps (double progression basis)**
- Plotkin et al. 2022 (PeerJ, n=43 trained, 8 weeks, 4 sets × 4 lower-body exercises, 2×/week, sets to failure): a LOAD group (add weight, fixed reps) and a REPS group (add reps, fixed weight) both worked. Rectus femoris hypertrophy modestly favoured REPS (2.8 mm [−0.5, 5.8]); dynamic strength slightly favoured LOAD (2.0 kg [−2.4, 7.8]). "Both… appear to be viable" — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/14142); [PubMed 36199287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)
- A commentary flags that sets went to failure and the population was specific, which limits generalizability — [Stanford Lifestyle Medicine](https://lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/increasing-weight-or-increasing-reps-can-both-make-you-stronger/)

**Autoregulation: RPE/RIR vs %1RM**
- Helms et al. 2018 (Front Physiol, 8-week DUP, trained males, n=21): RPE-based and %1RM loading both increased squat/bench 1RM and muscle thickness with no significant difference. RPE had a 57–79% chance of a small 1RM advantage vs 4–14% for %1RM. Hypertrophy was similar. %1RM loading can misfire when tested 1RMs are off or when rep capacity at a given % varies between people — [PubMed 29628895](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/f609bea1-40fd-4841-a17f-4a5a1d6f8519); [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/pdf)
- Graham & Cleather (JSCR 2021;35:2451–2456; deposited 2019; 12-week front squat, RIR autoregulation vs fixed block loading): autoregulation gave greater strength adaptations. The AR group trained at a higher average intensity (83.3 ± 13.4% vs 80.4 ± 10.0% 1RM) — [St Mary's repository](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/id/eprint/3067); [dissertation PDF](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/id/eprint/1802/1/Dissertation%20-%20Timothy%20Graham.pdf)
- A 2022 systematic review found no significant pooled difference between autoregulated and standardized load prescription (reported via search summary; I could not identify the review's authors) — [Sports Med Open PDF s40798-021-00404-9](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9.pdf)

**Periodization**
- Williams et al. 2017 (Sports Med; 18 studies, 81 effects): periodized > non-periodized for 1RM, ES = 0.43 (95% CI 0.27–0.58), "moderate". Most studies lasted under 16 weeks — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs40279-017-0734-y); [PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf)
- Moesgaard et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:1647–1666; 35 studies; volume-equated): periodized > non-periodized for 1RM, ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–0.57). Undulating > linear for 1RM, ES 0.31, driven by trained participants (ES 0.61), not untrained ones. No hypertrophy difference (LP vs UP ES 0.05). Too few studies to meta-analyse block periodization or other models — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1.pdf)
- Grgic et al. 2017 (PeerJ): linear and DUP likely produce similar hypertrophy — [PMC5571788](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5571788/); [Stronger By Science summary (practitioner)](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-periodization-style/)
- An earlier 2015 meta-analysis found no linear-vs-undulating strength difference (reported in Moesgaard search summary) — [PubMed 25268290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/)

**Deloads**
- Bell et al. 2023 Delphi (Sports Med Open; 34→29→21 strength/physique coaches): consensus definition of deloading is a period of reduced training stress to limit physiological and psychological fatigue, aid recovery and prepare for later training. Usually done by cutting volume (fewer reps per set and/or fewer sets) — [PMC10511399](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10511399/); [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/32417)
- Rogerson, Bell et al. 2024 cross-sectional survey (Sports Med Open; n=246 competitive strength/physique athletes): a deload lasted 6.4 ± 1.7 days, about every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks. Volume dropped via fewer reps per set and fewer sets per week; frequency and exercise selection stayed the same. Main motives were fatigue and energy management; deloads were also triggered by stalled performance or more soreness and joint aches — [PMC10948666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10948666); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y)
- De Marco et al. 2024 (S&C coaches across sports): most cut volume during deloads, most commonly by 0–25% (via search summary) — [PubMed 39446750](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39446750/)
- Coleman et al. 2024 (PeerJ; n=39 trained; 9-week high-volume program): a 1-week complete training cessation at the midpoint *reduced* lower-body isometric and dynamic strength gains vs continuous training. It had no effect on hypertrophy, power or local endurance, and continuous training gave slightly better readiness-to-train — [PeerJ 16777](https://peerj.com/articles/16777); [PubMed 38274324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)

**Exercise variation**
- Kassiano et al. 2022 (JSCR 36(6):1753–1762, systematic review): systematic variation can improve regional hypertrophy and maximize dynamic strength. Excessive or random variation may compromise gains, and redundant exercises and very frequent swapping may impair adaptation. Variation should follow anatomical and biomechanical reasoning — [PubMed 35438660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)
- Practitioner commentary: manage variation carefully for skill-heavy lifts (e.g. squat); varying other exercises likely won't hurt strength — [Biolayne](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-7/exercise-variation-for-strength-and-hypertrophy-change-is-not-always-good/)
- Kassiano and colleagues (2024, Res Q Exerc Sport): varied and fixed routines gave virtually similar strength and hypertrophy (via search summary) — [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/4912)

### Inferences
Candidate rules for the generator:
- **Rule B1: double progression as the default for accessory and hypertrophy work (Moderate-High).** Prescribe a rep range (e.g. 8–12). When all sets reach the top of the range at the target RIR, increase the load and reset to the bottom. Plotkin 2022 supports both rep and load progression as valid; the specific range logic is a convention, not directly tested.
- **Rule B2: load progression for main strength lifts (Moderate).** Plotkin 2022 shows a slight strength trend for load progression.
- **Rule B3: RIR-based autoregulation with e1RM tracking (Moderate).** Have users log reps and RIR, compute e1RM, and select the next load to hit the target reps at the target RIR. Prefer this to fixed %1RM from a single test (Helms 2018; Graham & Cleather). Evidence suggests it is equal to or slightly better than fixed loading, not dramatically better.
- **Rule B4: model choice by level and goal (Moderate).**
  - Beginners: simple linear or session-to-session progression; periodization style matters little (the UP advantage appears only in trained lifters).
  - Trained + strength goal: undulating intensity across the week (Moesgaard ES 0.61 in trained).
  - Any hypertrophy goal: periodization style is irrelevant if volume is equal (Moesgaard; Grgic). Prioritize volume and effort.
  - Block periodization: insufficient meta-analytic evidence. Low confidence for any specific advantage.
- **Rule B5: deloads (Low-Moderate).**
  - Default: a reduced-volume (not zero-training) deload week every ~4–8 weeks (practice norm of 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks).
  - Keep frequency and exercises; cut sets and/or reps.
  - Also trigger early on performance stalls or rising soreness or joint pain.
  - Do not prescribe full week-off layoffs for strength-focused users (Coleman 2024).
  - Most evidence is practice and consensus, not RCT outcome data.
- **Rule B6: exercise rotation (Moderate).**
  - Keep main or skill-heavy lifts stable across mesocycles.
  - Rotate accessory exercises in a structured way, e.g. per mesocycle (about every 4–8 weeks), choosing variants that target different regions or angles.
  - Never randomize session to session (Kassiano 2022).
  - The specific rotation interval is an inference, not a tested parameter.
- **Rule B7: plateau handling (Low; inferential).**
  - Trigger: e1RM for a lift flat or declining over about 2–3 consecutive exposures, or RIR consistently lower than planned at the same load.
  - Response, in order: (1) apply a deload; (2) if the stall persists, swap the exercise variant (structured variation); (3) for trained lifters, change the rep-range or intensity distribution (undulation); (4) for hypertrophy, consider adding sets or advanced techniques (Krzysztofik 2019 positions advanced techniques for plateaus).
  - The thresholds are design choices; no study validates them.

### Gaps
- **Load increments by exercise type:** I found no peer-reviewed source specifying increments (e.g. 2.5 kg upper / 5 kg lower, % based). The generator should treat these as practitioner convention, with a micro-loading fallback when an increment exceeds ~5% of load.
- **Double progression vs fixed loads:** I found no direct RCT on double progression as a named method; Plotkin 2022 is the closest.
- **Baz-Valle 2019:** not retrieved in this pass.
- **Plateau detection:** no validated algorithmic stall-detection criteria found in the literature.
- **Deload timing:** no RCT compares time-based with performance-triggered deloads. Coleman 2024 tested only full cessation, not a reduced-volume deload.
- **2022 autoregulation systematic review:** authors and pooled numbers were not verified from the full text.
- **Grgic 2017:** the pooled effect size was not retrieved.
